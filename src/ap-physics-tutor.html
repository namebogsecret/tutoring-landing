AP Physics C is the calculus-based end of the AP physics family, and most marks are lost where the mathematics and the physics meet: setting up a derivative or an integral from a physical situation, keeping signs and units under control, and writing a free-response answer that shows the reasoning rather than only a final number. Once the exam structure is clear, all of this can be prepared for in a planned way.

Two courses, two exams

College Board describes AP Physics C as a calculus-based, college-level physics course, especially suited to students planning to specialise or major in the physical sciences or engineering. It is offered as two separate courses, each with its own exam:

  • AP Physics C: Mechanics, which runs from kinematics through forces, energy, momentum, rotation and oscillations.
  • AP Physics C: Electricity and Magnetism, which runs from electric fields and Gauss's law through potential, capacitors and circuits to magnetic fields and induction.

The two exams have the same format. Because the courses are separate, they can be prepared one after the other or in parallel, depending on the school timetable and the exam dates. AP Physics 1 and AP Physics 2 are neighbouring courses and are not the subject of this page.

How each exam is built

According to the College Board exam pages, both Physics C exams, effective from the May 2027 exam, consist of two sections of equal weight:

  • Section I, multiple choice — 42 questions, 85 minutes, 50% of the exam score.
  • Section II, free response — 4 questions, 95 minutes, 50% of the exam score.

Calculators are permitted. Half of the score therefore comes from written solutions, which is why technique matters as much as knowledge. College Board lists the four free-response questions as one of each of these types: mathematical routines, translation between representations, experimental design and analysis, and qualitative/quantitative translation.

📐

Half of the score is written. The free-response section is 4 questions in 95 minutes, which is roughly 24 minutes per question. A solution that is correct but disorganised is hard to finish in that time, so layout and the order of steps are trained from the first lessons, not in the last weeks.

Mechanics: the seven units

The multiple-choice section of AP Physics C: Mechanics is weighted by unit, and the ranges below are the ones published by College Board.

  1. Kinematics — 10–15%
  2. Force and Translational Dynamics — 20–25%
  3. Work, Energy, and Power — 15–25%
  4. Linear Momentum — 10–20%
  5. Torque and Rotational Dynamics — 10–15%
  6. Energy and Momentum of Rotating Systems — 10–15%
  7. Oscillations — 10–15%

Forces and energy together carry the largest share, so a plan usually spends the most time there, and then uses rotation and oscillations to connect everything learned before. These are ranges, so they are a guide to emphasis and not an exact count of questions.

Electricity and Magnetism: the six units

The units of AP Physics C: Electricity and Magnetism continue the same numbering, and the multiple-choice weighting published by College Board is as follows.

  1. Electric Charges, Fields, and Gauss's Law — 15–25%
  2. Electric Potential — 10–20%
  3. Conductors and Capacitors — 10–15%
  4. Electric Circuits — 15–25%
  5. Magnetic Fields and Electromagnetism — 10–20%
  6. Electromagnetic Induction — 10–20%

Here the calculus is more visible than in Mechanics: fields from continuous charge distributions, potential as an integral of the field, flux and induction. Fields, Gauss's law and circuits are the heaviest units, and induction ties the electric and magnetic parts together at the end of the course.

Where the calculus meets the physics

Students usually do not struggle with the calculus in isolation. The difficulty is the translation: deciding what to differentiate with respect to what, choosing the integration variable and limits, and noticing when a symmetry makes the integral trivial. The same applies to the mathematical-routines question type in the free-response section, where a derivation is expected, not a numerical answer. Lessons therefore put the physical situation first and the mathematics second, with the mathematical steps written out in full. The tutor holds a perfect 200/200 on the US Praxis Mathematics exam as well as on Praxis Physics, which helps when a physics problem stops at a piece of mathematics.

Free-response technique

Free-response answers reward a particular way of writing: state the principle or law, write the relation in symbols, substitute, and only then compute, keeping units and the sign convention visible throughout. Practice is built around this:

  • Symbols before numbers. Derive the result algebraically first. It is shorter, easier to check, and earns marks even when the arithmetic later slips.
  • Representations. Practise moving between a verbal description, a diagram, a graph and an equation, which is exactly what the translation-between-representations question asks for.
  • Experimental design. Practise describing what is measured, what is varied, what is held constant and how a graph would be linearised to extract a quantity.
  • Qualitative reasoning. Practise short, precise explanations that link a physical claim to the principle behind it, and quantitative-qualitative questions that ask for both.

How the work is organised

  • Diagnostic. A set of questions across the units of the chosen course shows what is secure, what is only memorised and where the calculus breaks the solution.
  • Plan by unit. The units are scheduled against the exam date, with more time on the units that carry the largest published weight.
  • Lessons and practice. One-to-one online lessons with a shared whiteboard, worked examples, then free-response-style problems written out as they would be on the paper.
  • Multiple choice. Timed sets for Section I, with every error sorted into a reason: a concept gap, a calculation slip or a misread question.
  • Timed sections. Full Section I and Section II under exam timing (85 and 95 minutes), followed by a line-by-line review of the lost marks.

No results are promised: they depend on the starting point, the time available and regular work between lessons.
